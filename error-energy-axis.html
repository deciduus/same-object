The Cost of Being Right

Three canonical bounds — published in 1948, 1961 and 2015 — all measure energy in units of kT, and are cited by communities that do not read each other. Put on one axis, every substrate that stores, transmits or checks information lands somewhere on the same scale.

Compiled 3 September 2026 kT(300 K) = 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J Landauer = kT ln2 = 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ J Draft — verification in progress

The identity that makes one axis possible

Two of the three bounds turn out to be the same number, and the third is its close relative.

Three bounds, one unit

Landauer (1961) E ≥ kT ln2 per bit erased  =  0.693 kT
Shannon (1948) Eb ≥ N₀ ln2, and for thermal noise N₀ = kT  →  0.693 kT
Error correction (2015) ΔW ≥ kT ln(ηeq/η) per error suppressed  →  2.303 kT per decade

The Shannon limit of −1.59 dB is ln 2 in linear units. When the channel noise is thermal, its spectral density is kT — so the minimum energy to send a bit reliably and the minimum energy to erase one are the same quantity. The 2015 bound extends it: suppressing error by a factor of ten costs at least kT·ln10.

Whether the bit is transmitted, erased, or checked, the currency is kT. That is why the substrates below can be plotted together at all — and why nobody has done it is a question about citation practice, not about physics.

What the axis shows

Two clusters, and a void between them

Molecular processes sit within a few hundred times the thermodynamic floor. Neural and electronic systems sit six to ten orders above it. Roughly three orders of magnitude in between are unoccupied.

What separates them is not chemistry. Near-Landauer systems are single-molecule, slow, and local. Far-from-Landauer systems are fast, and move charge across structures thousands of times larger than the bit they represent. A neuron and a transistor are on the same side of that line — which is probably why they land together.

The brain is in the far cluster

A cortical synapse and a 45 nm CMOS adder agree within a factor of 1.6. Four billion years of separation, every implementation detail different, same position on the axis.

So the brain's advantage over silicon is architectural — connectivity, compute-in-memory, sparsity — not per-operation thermodynamic efficiency. At this level biology has no edge. And neuromorphic hardware, despite the biological framing, sits at ~10⁹ — no closer to the bound than an ordinary supercomputer FLOP.

Reliability does not explain the gap

The obvious candidate: being right costs energy, and the bound is kT·ln(1/ε). At a demanding digital error rate of 10⁻¹⁵ that comes to about 50× Landauer.

The observed gap is 10⁶. Reliability accounts for under a third of it in log terms. The most-cited explanation for why real systems sit far above the floor is quantitatively insufficient by four orders of magnitude — and as far as this survey found, nobody has said so.

What is left: the finite-time cost of fast operation, and the fact that both far-cluster substrates move ~10³–10⁴ carriers across structures vastly larger than the bit. If that is the answer, the convergence is architectural — both are wire-dominated — rather than a deep law.

Why this figure did not already exist

The measurement, not the argument.

All 1,463 papers citing the founding kinetic-proofreading paper were pulled and their reference lists intersected against Shannon.

The sixteen that do cite Shannon cite him for entropy, never for the channel coding theorem. And the sharpest detail: 33 papers in the proofreading literature use the word "decoding," and every one means the ribosome's A-site. The vocabulary collides exactly. The formalism never meets.

For contrast, DNA data storage — where the comparison is routinely made — co-cites error-correcting codes at 5.4%, ten times the proofreading rate, and reports its results as a fraction of Shannon capacity. That is what a field with a shared axis sounds like.

So this is not a gap that lacks a name. It lacks a citation path. Three canonical bounds — 1948, 1961, 2015 — all in kT, cited by communities that do not read one another.
