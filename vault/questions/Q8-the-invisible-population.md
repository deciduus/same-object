# Metrology has a standardised way of not resolving disagreements

> The selection bias in [[C7-discrepancy-catalogue]] is not only that quiet resolutions are
> hard to find. **There is an entire population of disagreements that is never framed as a
> disagreement at all** — and it is large, indexed, and public.

## What was found

BIPM key comparisons routinely produce results where participating national labs disagree beyond
their stated uncertainties. CCQM-K143: about 20 national metrology institutes, Cochran's Q
rejects homogeneity, "many do not have overlapping uncertainties."

**And no paper says the methods disagreed.** The excess variance is absorbed into the reference
value by a random-effects estimator — DerSimonian–Laird, or the NIST Consensus Builder.

That is a **standardised procedure for not resolving a disagreement.** It is good metrology. It
also makes the entire class invisible to any catalogue built from headline claims.

## The same mechanism, higher up

It is not confined to inter-lab comparisons. The same move appears in the constants themselves,
as an **expansion factor** applied inside a CODATA adjustment:

| Quantity | Expansion factor | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Newton's constant G | **3.9** | the inputs disagree by ~4× their stated bars |
| α (Cs vs Rb) | 2.5 | applied to **both**, so neither side is blamed |
| Tenth-order QED coefficient | 2.1 | **two calculations of the same number, 4.8σ apart** |
| R_∞ / r_p / r_d | 1.6 | |
| h, for the kilogram redefinition | 1.7 | then frozen by fiat in 2019 |

**A disagreement recorded as an expansion factor leaves no trace a search can find.** There is
no paper titled *our tenth-order coefficient disagrees with theirs*; it is a figure caption and
a multiplier.

## Why this is a door, not just a caveat

It changes what the base-rate project *is*. The question was "of persistent disagreements, how
many were real?" — and it presumed the population was the set of famous puzzles.

**The real population is much larger and better documented**, just written in a different
register: expansion factors in adjustment papers, and homogeneity statistics in key-comparison
reports. Those are systematic, published on a schedule, and cover thousands of measurements.

## The question

**Can the base rate be computed from expansion factors and key-comparison statistics instead of
from famous cases?** That population is nearly fame-free — nobody becomes known for a
DerSimonian–Laird adjustment — which removes the bias that made [[C7-discrepancy-catalogue]]'s
headline number untrustworthy.

The trade is that these records usually do not say *which side was wrong*, and often the case
never resolves at all. So it answers a slightly different question: not *how did it end*, but
**how often does a discipline conclude that it cannot tell.**

That may be the more useful number, and it is the one this project can actually get.

## The other thing found here

**The proton radius case has two official answers.** CODATA-2022 says "the proton radius 'puzzle'
is not yet over" at 2.8σ; the 2026 review literature headlines that there is no difference.
Whichever a catalogue picks changes the OUTCOME field.

So an outcome label is not just perishable — see [[C7-discrepancy-catalogue]] on muon g−2 — it is
**contested at any given moment**. Any base rate needs an as-of date *and* a stated authority.
