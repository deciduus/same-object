# What decides whether a disputed claim gets checked?

> The refuted feature left something better behind. *Number of groups* predicts nothing.
> **Cost and motivation of the check** appears to predict everything — and nobody has measured it.

## Where this came from

[[G9-discrepancy-base-rate]] claimed single-group results usually resolve against the claimant.
Refuted — Homestake, CP violation 1964, Wu 1957 are all vindicated single-group claims.

But the counterexample hunt handed back a **replacement hypothesis**, which is the interesting part:

| Case | Was it checked? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **DAMA** | Yes — ANAIS-112 and COSINE-100, purpose-built, same target | **Refuting it was publishable** |
| **Homestake** | Not for ~20 years | The check required a mine |

Two long-running single-group claims, opposite treatment. The difference is not the evidence.
It is **what the checking experiment costs and what career it earns.**

## The question, sharpened

Is there a measurable quantity — call it *checkability* — combining apparatus cost, time to
result, and the citation value of a refutation, that predicts whether a disputed claim gets
independently tested?

And the sharper version: **does a field's replication rate track the publishability of negative
results rather than the importance of the claims?**

## Why it might be answerable

Both inputs are public. Replication attempts are countable. Apparatus costs are in funding
records and instrumentation papers. Citation counts for refutations versus confirmations are
directly measurable.

This is the same shape as the base rate in [[G9-discrepancy-base-rate]] and could be computed
from the same catalogue, at little extra cost.

## What it would change

If checkability predicts replication better than importance does, then **an unchecked claim is
weak evidence of anything** — and the corollary G9 relied on ("a decade of silence is the
result") inverts into a warning about the field rather than about the claim.
